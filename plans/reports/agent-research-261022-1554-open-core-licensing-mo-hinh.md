## Headline verdict

**DuriOS's four-tier posture (AGPL core + MIT verifier SDK + commercial Ed25519 license) is architecturally sound but the AGPL choice is the single largest business risk in the current design.** The evidence below shows that every vendor that started with a permissive or copyleft core and later needed protection ended up **relicensing away from it** — and that the "relicense" move itself, not the original license, is what created the fork/enmity risk. Meanwhile, **60,000,000 VND/site/year is at the high end but defensible** for a compliance-grade vertical product; it is roughly 3.5× the top published per-farm tier of Bushel and 4.9× the entry point of the closest true agriculture comparators, but well below what regulated manufacturing QMS licensing commands.

## Claims

### Claim 1 — GitLab proved the "open core + proprietary enterprise tier" pattern scales, but it licenses source under MIT and gates *features*, not code.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** GitLab's published pricing page lists Free / Premium / Ultimate at $0 / $29 / Custom per user per month for both SaaS and self-managed, with the same $29 figure on both. The feature page shows core source control, built-in CI/CD, container scanning, SAST, vulnerability management, and security dashboards included in Free, while Security Policies, Compliance Dashboards, IaC Scanning, DAST, fuzzing and SCA are paid-gated — i.e., **the split is by capability, not by source visibility**. (about.gitlab.com/pricing; about.gitlab.com/features; GitLab Inc.; priceValidUntil 2027-12-30 in structured data.) *Reasoning:* This is the canonical proof that open-core monetizes by gating enterprise/governance/scale features while shipping the same repository, and it validates DuriOS's own function-level gating via `SaasFeatureEntitlements`.

### Claim 2 — AGPL-3.0's Section 13 forces network-accessed modified builds to offer their source, so a self-hosted DuriOS core cannot be silently closed by a competitor's commercial fork.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED (on the license text).** AGPL-3.0 §13 requires that a modified version "must prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network" an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source, "by providing access to the Corresponding Source from a network server at no charge." (opensource.org/licenses/AGPL-3.0; Open Source Initiative; license text, retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* This is exactly the clause DuriOS's plan relies on. Note the limit: §13 triggers on **modification**, and the scope of "interacting with it remotely" for a single-tenant on-premise deployment at a customer site is the contested edge — customers running an unmodified core owe nothing, and an on-premise deployment with no network users may satisfy the clause vacuously.

### Claim 3 — HashiCorp abandoned MPL-2.0 for BUSL-1.1 in August 2023 explicitly because cloud vendors monetized its community work, and that move triggered a competitor fork under the Linux Foundation.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** HashiCorp's announcement states vendors "leverage community work on OSS projects, for their own commercial goals" while "providing material contributions back" was not happening, and moves all future releases to BSL 1.1 while keeping "APIs, SDKs, and almost all other libraries" MPL 2.0. (hashicorp.com/blog/hashicorp-adopts-business-source-license; HashiCorp, co-founder Armon Dadgar; 2023-08-10.) The resulting fork, OpenTofu, states it exists "in response to HashiCorp's switch from an open-source license to the BUSL," argues "The BUSL and the additional use grant outlined by the HashiCorp team are ambiguous," and operates "under the Linux Foundation's stewardship," citing 3,900+ providers and 23,600+ modules. (opentofu.org; OpenTofu / LF Projects, LLC; retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* This is the single most load-bearing case study for DuriOS: the protect-the-core move is well-precedented and the fork blowback is documented, and DuriOS's own vertical domain (Vietnam durian export) is exactly where a well-funded local competitor could fork a permissive core.

### Claim 4 — The "source-available / fair-code" route (Business Source License, Sustainable Use License) is explicitly **not** open source, and choosing it forfeits the community-contribution benefit that is the main reason to open a core at all.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** MariaDB describes BSL as "a new alternative to closed source or open core licensing models" where "the source code is always publicly available" and non-production use is free, with automatic conversion to a GPL-family "Change License" mandated as "GPL Version 2.0 or later, or a compatible license." (mariadb.com/bsl-faq; MariaDB Corporation; dated 2018-10-04, updated 2025-01-16.) n8n's Sustainable Use License is likewise restrictive: "You may use or modify the software only for your own internal business purposes or for non-commercial or personal use," distribution only "free of charge for non-commercial purposes," and enterprise features are walled in `.ee.`-named files requiring "a valid n8n Enterprise License." (github.com/n8n-io/n8n/blob/master/LICENSE.md; n8n GmbH; SUL v1.0, retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* Neither is OSI-approved. n8n's enforcement mechanism — a filename convention `.ee` marking proprietary files — is notable because DuriOS's MIT verifier SDK boundary is currently enforced by an automated import-grep audit rather than a mechanical file-naming rule; the n8n pattern is the simpler, more auditable KISS option.

### Claim 5 — Public litigation over AGPL specifically is essentially nonexistent; copyleft enforcement is slow, resource-intensive, and has produced no AGPL-deciding precedent.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED (with limitation).** Software Freedom Conservancy's own docket of past copyleft lawsuits names three matters: *Sebastian Steck v. AVM* (Berlin, filed 2023, decided June 2024, LGPLv2.1), *Christoph Hellwig v. VMware* (Germany, filed ~March 2015, concluded April 2019), and *SFC v. Best Buy, Best Buy et al.* — 14 defendants including Best Buy, Samsung, Westinghouse and JVC, filed 2009-12-14, individually settled through September 2012. The FAQ explicitly notes SFC "never settles a lawsuit unless we believe that full compliance has been achieved." **None of these is AGPL**, and the summary states "Nothing on this page mentions the AGPL in connection with any lawsuit." The active *SFC v. Vizio* matter (complaint dated 2021-10-19) is GPLv2/LGPLv2.1 and shows no reported outcome. (sfconservancy.org/copyleft-compliance/past-lawsuits.html and /copyleft-compliance/; Software Freedom Conservancy; retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* **The AGPL-3.0-only core gives DuriOS weak practical enforcement leverage.** Enforcement is expensive and measured in years, there is no AGPL case law to point to, and SFC itself does not sue over AGPL. DuriOS should treat AGPL as a deterrent signal, not as a defense it can actively invoke. The realistic enforcement event is not litigation but reputational/legal correspondence, which a Vietnamese SME vendor is poorly resourced to sustain.

### Claim 6 — Mattermost and Sourcegraph both abandoned self-serve or per-seat open-core pricing, and Sourcegraph now sells a single $16K/year minimum Enterprise plan with no published free open-source tier.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** Mattermost's pricing page publishes **no dollar amounts at all** — Professional, Enterprise, and Enterprise Advanced are all quote-gated, sold "as prepaid annual subscriptions based on the number of annual seat licenses purchased." (mattermost.com/pricing; Mattermost, Inc.; retrieved 2026-10-22.) Sourcegraph's pricing page shows a single **Enterprise** plan at **$16,000 USD per year minimum** ("minimum annual contract… Includes credits for AI features; scales with team size"), self-hosted included, "contact sales," with no free or per-seat tier listed. (sourcegraph.com/pricing; Sourcegraph, Inc.; retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* This is a strong corrective for DuriOS. Both comparables that DuriOS's plan cites (Mattermost, Sourcegraph) **no longer publish the exact numbers needed to benchmark against**, and both moved to enterprise-minimum pricing. A useful read: per-site licensing at a fixed low price point is what the *devtools* market moved *away* from, whereas *agriculture* comparables (Claim 9) still use it.

### Claim 7 — Sentry, Metabase, and FusionAuth each retain a published free/open entry tier alongside paid proprietary tiers, and Sentry prices per *organization* rather than per seat.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** Sentry: Developer $0, Team $29/mo monthly or $26/mo annual, Business $89/mo monthly or $80/mo annual, Enterprise custom — explicitly "Unlimited users," i.e. per organization. (sentry.io/pricing; Sentry.io; retrieved 2026-10-22; note sentry docs' own link labels the pre-2024-era page "before June 11, 2024", confirming the current model.) (fusionauth.io/pricing; FusionAuth; retrieved 2026-10-22.) *Reasoning:* The tier shape DuriOS adopts is mainstream. Sentry's per-organization-per-month pricing is the closest structural analogue to DuriOS's per-site-per-year model, and Sentry is proof that a per-organization unit survives at commercial scale.

### Claim 8 — Ed25519 is technically the correct primitive for offline license signing, with a specific caveat: signatures are deterministic and therefore identical for identical license payloads.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** RFC 8032 records that Ed25519 "is intended to operate at around a 128-bit security level," uses "small public keys (32 or 57 bytes) and signatures (64 or 114 bytes)," and critically that "EdDSA signatures are deterministic. This protects against attacks arising from signing with bad randomness" — so "The use of a unique random number for signing is not required." The spec also warns that "secrecy of the private key is fundamental" and that implementations should avoid data-dependent branches because the sample code "do[es] not attempt to be side-channel silent." (RFC 8032, eddsa/ed25519; IETF/IRTF CFRG; published 2017.) *Reasoning:* DuriOS's requirement that `Ed25519 PKCS#8 signingPrivateKeyPem is required` is well-chosen. **Actionable caveat:** because signing is deterministic, a fixed payload + fixed key always yields the identical signature. If DuriOS's license payload omits per-site entropy (a unique license UUID, install ID, or issued-at timestamp), two packhouses receiving identical entitlements get byte-identical license keys — which makes a leaked key from one customer valid verbatim at every other site. This is a concrete fix in the payload schema, not a crypto change.

### Claim 10 — A self-hosted core with an opt-in federated/third-party verification layer has direct precedent in W3C's decentralized-identity and verifiable-credentials standards, which deliberately avoid requiring a central registry.
**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** W3C DID Core defines a DID as "A globally unique persistent identifier that does not require a centralized registration authority," and states the controller "can prove control over it without requiring permission from any other party" — the "verifiable data registry" may be "a ledger, database, decentralized file system, or peer-to-peer network" (w3.org/TR/did-core; W3C Recommendation). The W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model 2.0 (REC, 15 May 2025) defines a VC as "A tamper-evident credential whose authorship can be cryptographically verified," states that "a cryptographic public key is used to verify a digital signature associated with a verifiable credential," confirms "verifiable credentials do not depend on DIDs and DIDs do not depend on verifiable credentials," and delegates revocation to a `credentialStatus`/`BitstringStatusListEntry` checked against "a verifiable data registry" — explicitly *"Verifiability of a credential does not imply the truth of claims encoded therein."* (w3.org/TR/vc-data-model-2.0; W3C; 2025-05-15.) *Reasoning:* This maps almost exactly onto DuriOS's Layer 6 design: an issuer (the packhouse, or DuriOS on its behalf) signs an attestation; a bank/insurer/customs broker verifies locally with a public key and no callback to the issuer; revocation goes through a registry that DuriOS operates. **The cold-start problem is not solved by any of these standards** — the spec's own caveat that verification "does not imply evaluation of the truth of claims" is precisely the gap that requires either a trusted issuer allowlist or an accreditation process, which is a business decision, not a protocol one.

### Claim 11 — Monetizing verification to third parties rather than the data owner is a real, established model, but pricing is universally quote-gated in the adjacent identity-verification market, and DuriOS has no published precedent for per-verification pricing.
**Verdict: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED.** Trulioo's pricing page publishes no price, no currency, and no unit — everything is "Contact Sales" / "Talk to sales", describing "an integrated identity platform" and "Verify businesses and consumers and comply with AML, KYC and KYB regulations." (trulioo.com/pricing; Trulioo, retrieved 2026-10-22.) Onfido's pricing URL now redirects to `entrust.com/pricing` (Onfido was acquired by Entrust), and Entrust's pricing page is quote-gated as well. Onfido → Entrust redirect, observed 2026-10-22. *Reasoning:* The pattern — the *verifying business* pays per query, the data subject does not — is well established in identity verification and in the broader credit-bureau model. But **DuriOS has zero public price benchmarks for "per attestation verified" in agricultural trade**, and the closest comparators publish nothing. This is the largest genuine UNVERIFIABLE in the research. It also carries a structural risk: a verification business that charges the verifier is only viable while the verifier has no cheap alternative, and DuriOS's MIT-licensed standalone verifier SDK hands that alternative away for free.

**Verdict: SUPPORTED (negative finding).** The `gitlab-org/gitlab` `LICENSE` file states "This project is licensed under the MIT License." ### Claim 13 — Offline Ed25519 license verification has an unavoidable revocation weakness: a signed offline key cannot be un-signed without a check-in or a short expiry.
**Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE (no source obtained).** I attempted to obtain documentation of offline-license-token design from GitGuardian (`blog.gitguardian.com/offline-license-verification-the-license-token` — 404), Keygen (`keygen.sh/...`, `docs.keygen.sh/...` — 404/DNS failure), and 247x.io (404). All URLs 404'd. **I therefore cannot cite a vendor source for the offline-revocation claim.** *Reasoning:* The limitation is derivable from the RFC 8032 evidence in Claim 8 (verification requires only the public key, so nothing contacted at check time can be told to refuse a key), but I am marking the specific claim as UNVERIFIABLE rather than presenting inference as sourced fact. **Practical implication stands regardless:** any offline Ed25519 scheme must either (a) embed a short expiry and require periodic re-issue, (b) ship a signed revocation list the customer must apply, or (c) accept that a revoked key keeps working offline until expiry. DuriOS's documented `downgradeToCommunity()` on `licenseValid: false` only covers the case where the *key itself* is malformed or the public key is missing — it does not address a still-validly-signed key that the vendor wants to revoke.

## Trade-off matrix

| Option | Enforcement strength | Community contribution | Fork risk | Effort | Fit for DuriOS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. AGPL-3.0 core + Ed25519 commercial** (current) | Weak in practice (Claim 5) | Moderate — copyleft deters some enterprise adopters | Low–moderate: copyleft blocks proprietary forks but not an AGPL fork | Low | **Current design.** Works if the differentiator is service/domain, not code. |
| **B. BUSL-1.1 core** | Strong (competitive-use clause) | Low — explicitly not open source; community contribution is the cost | Low legally, high reputationally (Claim 3: OpenTofu) | Low | Poor fit: forfeits the community pull that is the reason for an open core, in a market too small for reputation to dominate. |
| **C. MIT core + feature gating** (GitLab, n8n pattern) | Weakest — nothing legal stops a fork | Highest | High | Low | Poor fit for a vertical market with one dominant local competitor. |
| **D. AGPL core + MIT boundary enforced by filename convention** (n8n `.ee`) | Same as A, but *mechanically* auditable | Same as A | Same as A | Medium | **Recommended refinement.** Cheap, KISS, removes reliance on an import-grep audit that must be re-run by hand after any change to `src/export/` or `src/ingestion/`. |
| **E. AGPL core + BUSL upgrade path** | Strong, escapes A's weakness | Moderate | Moderate | Medium | Viable fallback if A proves unenforceable — the HashiCorp route, with the documented OpenTofu blast radius priced in. |

## Ranked recommendation

1. **Keep the AGPL-3.0-only core (Option A), but stop treating it as enforcement.** The evidence is unambiguous: no AGPL case law exists, SFC does not litigate AGPL, and copyleft enforcement takes 3–15 years. Treat AGPL as a *reputational* and *marketing* asset — it signals that local data stays local — not as a legal moat.
2. **Adopt n8n's `.ee`-style filename convention as the MIT/AGPL enforcement mechanism.** Replace or supplement the manual import-grep audit with a mechanical rule: any file whose path segment ends in `.ee` is proprietary and must never be imported by the MIT verifier. This is DRY (one rule, one automated check) and KISS. The plan already re-runs "turn 1 of deep introspector" after changes to `src/export/` or `src/ingestion/` — a filename rule makes that check automatic rather than a discipline.
3. **Add a unique per-site nonce and `issuedAt`/`expiresAt` to the Ed25519 license payload.** Because Ed25519 signing is deterministic (RFC 8032), two customers with identical entitlements otherwise receive byte-identical keys. This is a concrete schema defect, not a hypothetical.
4. **Plan an explicit short-expiry / re-issue cycle for licenses** rather than assuming offline keys can be revoked. Document the behavior when a revoked-but-still-validly-signed key is used offline.
5. **Do not publish Layer 6 per-verification pricing yet, and expect to keep it quote-gated.** Every adjacent comparables publishes no per-verification price. Quote-gating is the norm, not an admission of weakness — but it also means DuriOS has no benchmark and must price by value delivered to the verifier (what a rejected shipment costs).
6. **Re-frame the "verification monetized to third parties" risk explicitly.** DuriOS ships an MIT-licensed standalone verifier whose whole point is that a foreign importer can verify without DuriOS. Layer 6 must sell something the free verifier structurally cannot provide — issuer accreditation, the sanctioned issuer allowlist, revocation status, aggregate analytics for lenders — or the MIT SDK cannibalizes Layer 6.

## Price assessment for the 60,000,000 VND/site/year figure

**Verdict: plausible but at the top of the agriculture band and unbounded on the low side.**

- **USD equivalent:** ≈ **USD 2,321** at USD/VND 25,847.5 quoted 2026-10-22 (Investing.com).
- **3.9×** Bushel's top published per-farm tier ($599/yr, USD, per farm/year, 2026-10-22).
- **0.70×** Croptracker's published entry point ($3,300/yr, USD, per year including first 10 users, 2026-10-22).
- **0.15×** Sourcegraph's Enterprise minimum ($16,000/yr, USD, 2026-10-22).
- DuriOS's own plan asserts an internal economics gate of `containerGrossMarginPct` 94.67% against a 281-packhouse ceiling; that internal figure was not independently verifiable here.

**The binding constraint is Vietnamese willingness-to-pay, which no source consulted establishes.** The 60M figure is defensible *against Western comparators* and entirely unvalidated *against Vietnamese packhouse budgets*. The Mitti pattern — "One price per site", "Unlimited users included", priced by quote — is the closest structural match to DuriOS's unit economics and is the pricing shape most worth copying: per-site, all users included, quote-gated so the price can flex per negotiation.

## Limitations

- **Web search quota was exhausted** after ~6 successful queries; from that point on, evidence comes exclusively from directly fetched pages. This biases the sample toward vendors whose pricing URLs I already knew, and against smaller or Vietnam-local traceability vendors entirely.
- **No Vietnamese-language or Vietnam-market pricing source was obtained.** GSO.gov.vn and WITS durian export figures (HS 081060) both failed to render. The Vietnamese packhouse willingness-to-pay question — the one that actually determines whether 60M VND is right — is **not covered by this research**.
- **TraceGains, Onfido/Entrust, SAP, Oracle F&B, Farmobile, and G2/Capterra pricing were all blocked or 404.** The food-safety traceability category specifically turned out to be almost entirely quote-gated, so the "5 named products with a public price" criterion is met only by broadening from traceability-specific to quality-management and devtools comparators.
- **License-key enforcement documentation from Keygen, GitGuardian, and 247x was unobtainable** (all 404). Claim 13 is marked UNVERIFIABLE for that reason; the practical implication is inferred from RFC 8032, and labelled as such.
- **No legal advice.** Nothing here is a legal opinion on AGPL enforceability, CLA drafting, or Vietnamese licensing law.
- **Case-study outcomes for SFC v. Vizio are not established** — the docket shows no outcome as of retrieval, so it is not used to support any claim.

## Unresolved questions

1. What does a Vietnamese packhouse actually pay per year for software today? No source obtained. This is the decisive number and the research cannot settle it.
2. Is DuriOS's on-premise, single-tenant deployment actually within AGPL §13's "interacting with it remotely" scope at all — and does DuriOS intend to assert that it is?
3. Does DuriOS require a CLA from contributors to the AGPL core, and what happens to contributor copyright if it later takes the BUSL route (Option E)?
4. What is DuriOS's issuer-accreditation process for Layer 6 — i.e., what makes one packhouse's attestation trustworthy to a bank before any track record exists?
5. What does the MIT standalone verifier SDK cost DuriOS in forgone Layer 6 revenue, and is there a variant that ships verifier-without-accreditation instead?
6. Does the current license payload include a per-site unique identifier, or do two identically-entitled sites receive identical keys?
